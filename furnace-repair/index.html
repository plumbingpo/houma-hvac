A modern furnace runs a strict startup sequence, and knowing where the sequence breaks is most of the diagnosis. The thermostat calls, the inducer motor spins up to prove draft through a pressure switch, the hot surface igniter glows, the gas valve opens, the burners light, the flame sensor confirms fire, and only then does the blower deliver heat to the house. In Houma, LA, the failures cluster at predictable points: an oxide-coated flame sensor, a hot surface igniter cracked from years of thermal cycling, a pressure switch that will not close because the inducer is weak or the condensate trap on a high-efficiency model has clogged, or a control board with a burned relay. Each one stops the sequence cold, and each one mimics the others from the hallway.

Rather than guess, Houma HVAC tests the sequence stage by stage. We read igniter resistance, measure flame signal in microamps, check that the pressure switch responds to real inducer draft, confirm voltage at the gas valve, and test the blower and its capacitor. Because this is a fuel-burning appliance, the safety basics come with every visit: we inspect the heat exchanger, leak-test the gas connections, and verify flue draft. You cannot tell a furnace problem from a furnace hazard by looking at the thermostat. Before we leave, temperature rise is measured against the data plate so the fire and the airflow are balanced as the manufacturer intended.

Houma HVAC services natural gas, propane, and electric furnaces from all major brands, from standard 80 percent units to condensing high-efficiency models with their traps, drain lines, and dual pressure switches. Our trucks carry the parts that really fail, such as igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, capacitors, inducer assemblies, and blower motors, so a typical no-heat call ends with the house warming up. Why Hire a Professional for Furnace Repair?

Gas, open flame, high voltage, and a flue share one cabinet in a furnace, and the most dangerous failure is the one you cannot see: a damaged heat exchanger that lets combustion gases into the air the blower sends through your home. Every morning that delayed ignition keeps booming at startup, that exchanger takes more stress. The diagnostic side has its own pitfalls. When a furnace trips its high limit, the switch itself may be bad, or the furnace may be protecting itself from a clogged filter or a failing blower. Replacing the switch when the real issue is airflow removes a safety device and leaves the actual problem in place.

Our experienced technicians look at every symptom in the context of the whole furnace. We measure flame signal instead of assuming it, which keeps us from replacing a healthy sensor while a cracked igniter or weak gas valve goes unnoticed. Draft is confirmed at the pressure switch and at the flue. Temperature rise is checked against the data plate, catching airflow problems before they cut years off the heat exchanger. The gas side is leak-tested with instruments, never with a quick sniff. The result is a furnace proven safe over a full cycle, plus an honest opinion if the unit is getting close to the point where replacing it beats repairing it again.

Maintenance Tips for Furnace Repair

A few simple habits now, like regular filter changes and seasonal tune-ups, can add years to your system's life. Here are our expert recommendations:

  • Pay attention when the burners light, since a boom instead of a soft whoosh calls for service
  • Put in a fresh filter monthly through the heating season, the cheapest protection your furnace can get
  • Keep the condensate trap and drain clear on high-efficiency units to prevent lockouts
  • Have the furnace checked every autumn, while a weak igniter or fouled sensor is still an easy fix
  • Clear boxes, lint, and above all chlorine-based cleaners out of the furnace area
  • Put a carbon monoxide detector on every level and test them monthly
  • Avoid packing the utility room, because burners need open space for combustion air
